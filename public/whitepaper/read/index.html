Abstract. Numen is a public Layer-1 blockchain built on the Polkadot SDK (Substrate) that pairs per-
missionless Proof-of-Work block production with the Byzantine-fault-tolerant finality gadget GRANDPA,
so that anyone can mine anonymously while transactions become irreversible within about a minute. Its
work function is Proof of Scan (PoScan). PoScan trades opaque double-hashing for verifiable geometric
labour. Every nonce derives a full-width seed from the protocol version and pre_hash, which itself com-
mits to the parent block and the block author. The miner generates a 2,562-vertex asteroid mesh from
that seed, scans it through the spectral3d pipeline into a 23-dimensional shape spectrum, and quantizes
the spectrum on a mining-grade fine grid. Hashing the buckets with pre_hash gives a full-entropy work
value. The scan itself is the work: low-resolution, surrogate, and precomputation shortcuts are closed
by measurement or by construction, and the winning nonce doubles as a reconstructible on-chain arti-
fact. One block, one asteroid. Numen drops recognition semantics on purpose. It keeps the scan and
refuses the object-identity claim, because that claim cannot survive in a consensus path. Validators are
identity-bearing and coin-locked, with no slashing and, deliberately, no reward; an on-chain exemption list
makes entry invitation-based in the early network, and the stake threshold is priced so that buying finality
is arithmetically impossible. The chain ships full EVM compatibility through Frontier. Governance removes
sudo entirely: a project key (prime) can put code to a vote, pull the brakes, and keep two administrative
gates, but it can never move funds, and all of this is verifiable on-chain. We document the open limitations
plainly: v1 does not target FPGA/ASIC resistance, and no third-party audit has yet been performed.

1 Introduction and vision

1.1 Motivation: work you can look at

Proof-of-Work remains the only battle-tested way to let strangers mint blocks without permission,
identity, or stake. Yet the work itself is a lottery of opaque hashes: society sees the energy
bill but never the labour. The field has a recurring ambition called useful PoW. It asks whether
the computation can leave something behind. Attempts to make mining useful to the outside
world (protein folding, machine-learning training) consistently stall on three structural problems:
decentralized task supply, verification as expensive as the work, and the double-selling of re-
sults off-chain. The one direction that survives scrutiny is internal usefulness: let the work itself
produce an artifact the chain can own.
Numen adopts that honest, narrow definition. Every block is the trace of real geometric labour,
the deterministic sculpting and spectral scanning of a small asteroid, and the winning nonce
reconstructs that asteroid forever. At the same time, pure Nakamoto consensus leaves users
with only probabilistic finality; exchanges wait dozens of confirmations. Numen therefore pairs
open PoW block production with GRANDPA [2], the Substrate ecosystem’s BFT finality protocol.
An independent, coin-locked validator set operates it. Miner and validator are separate roles that
never mix.

1.2 The name, and what it promises

Numen is the Latin word for the divinity the Romans believed inhabited each concrete thing.
It is the animating presence of this particular spring, stone, or threshold. The name carries
two deliberate overtones. Noumenon is Kant’s thing-in-itself. Númenor is the island of the far-
sighted. It was chosen because it maps precisely onto the technical core. Numen turns the
three-dimensional shape of an object into a stable digital fingerprint; it gives each concrete thing
its own presence on-chain. Fan Zhen’s thesis is the design in one line: the form is the substance,
and the fingerprint is merely the form’s function; there is no separate soul to be forged apart from
the shape.
That promise comes with a discipline the rest of this paper keeps carefully. The mapping “shape
→ fingerprint” is real and load-bearing inside consensus; it is not a claim that Numen authenti-
cates physical objects in the world. Section 1.3 explains why that stronger claim is a dead end,
and Section 1.5 states exactly where the line falls.

1.3 Why recognition cannot be the work

There is a tempting way to put 3D scanning into a blockchain: let miners process real 3D objects,
treat an object’s recognition hash as its durable identity, and sell the result as “1 object = 1 asset.”
Numen studied this design space closely before rejecting it, because every property that makes
a recognition pipeline good at recognizing makes it wrong for consensus.
First, the mechanism problems that make recognition-as-consensus unsafe:

 • Perceptual hashing is anti-avalanche by design. A recognition hash must map similar
   shapes to the same value; mining needs the opposite. With identity-grade quantization the
   reachable work space collapses to a few thousand values. A laptop enumerates it in minutes,
   diﬀiculty loses meaning, and mining degenerates into a lookup table.
 • Work not bound to the block or the miner. An object’s hash has no parent hash inside it:
   it can be precomputed, replayed across blocks, or stolen from another miner’s submission.

• Miner-supplied objects invite degenerate and forged meshes. A 12-vertex sliver scans
   almost for free (work collapses), and a forge-collide experiment shows a 12-vertex mesh
   can impersonate the coarse-bucket identity of a 2,562-vertex object, producing counterfeit
   “winning shapes.”
 • Floating-point features sit in consensus. Recognition pipelines are f64 geometry; cross-
   platform math divergence at a bucket boundary is a consensus fork.
 • Shape-only identity is weaker than it looks. Silhouette-level features miss internal geome-
   try and collide across scaled or same-outline objects. The feature-entropy ceiling also makes
   the hash reversible to a coarse shape. That is bounded leakage, not zero-knowledge.

Second, and decisively, the product built on shape-only recognition does not survive contact with
reality. Contour-level recognition loses internal structure and is scale-sensitive, so distinct objects
with similar outlines collide, and a modified object with an unchanged silhouette still matches.
Copyright registration, luxury-goods authentication, physical-object identity, manufacturing veri-
fication: each intended application dies on that same defect. An authenticity oracle that a same-
silhouette replica can satisfy is not an oracle.
The honest conclusion is not that scanning has no place in consensus; it is that recognition output
cannot be the entropy source, and object authenticity cannot be the product.

1.4 The reframing: the scan is the work, not the identity

PoScan as specified here inverts every failing assumption of Section 1.3.

 • The chain, not the miner, chooses the object. The mesh comes from a seed the chain
   fixes, never from anything the miner supplies (Section 5.2). Trivial objects, forged objects,
   and replays all die at once, because verifiers regenerate the mesh and there is nothing to
   submit but a nonce.
 • Quantization is mining-grade, not identity-grade. The 23 spectral features are quantized
   about 105 times finer than recognition buckets (Section 5.4). At that resolution the channel
   stops being enumerable, and the final SHA-256 turns it into a uniform 256-bit work value.
 • Identity claims are simply dropped. The asteroid is an artifact, not an asset-authenticity
   oracle. Nothing in consensus asserts that two similar shapes are “the same thing”; the anti-
   avalanche property that poisoned mining is discarded rather than patched.

What survives from the scan-as-identity vision is exactly the part that was always sound: a scan
is honest, replayable geometric labour. Making the miner perform it, and making every shortcut
measurably fail, is the substance of Section 5.

1.5 What Numen claims, and refuses to claim

Numen is precise about its central sentence, because the difference between the true and the
tempting version is the difference between a defensible protocol and a marketing claim an expert
dismisses on sight.

We claim: block production requires executing the spectral3d scan of a chain-chosen
   mesh; the scan is the work; and any approximate or shortcut implementation computes
   a different value and forfeits the block. This rests on the bit-level determinism of a single
   reference implementation (Section 5.7).
   We do not claim: that shape “invariance” or noise robustness secures mining. Once the
   seed fixes the mesh, its pose is fixed; invariance plays no role in mining and is deliber-
   ately idle. The load-bearing property is avalanche. Any deviation yields a wildly different
   work value. That is the exact opposite of the recognition property. A white paper that sold
   invariance as the security base would be wrong, and we say so plainly.



2 Design principles
 • PoW decides who speaks; BFT decides what is final. Anyone may mine anonymously;
   irreversibility comes from an explicit vote of an accountable validator set.
 • Reorgs before finality are normal. The protocol does not fight short PoW races; it bounds
   them with fast finality.
 • Miners and validators are disjoint roles. No overlap is required or assumed; either side
   can fail without taking the other down.
 • Verification is cheap, deterministic, and runs in the runtime. Seal verification executes
   inside the wasm runtime (PowVerifyApi), so the PoW algorithm itself can be replaced by a
   forkless runtime upgrade.
 • Determinism before cleverness. Every consensus computation must reproduce bit-for-bit
   across x86-64, aarch64, and wasm32; anything that cannot is kept out of the consensus path.
 • EVM compatibility first. Developers and users keep their Ethereum tooling unchanged.
 • The project can ship code, pull the brakes, and keep gates. It can never move funds.
   No super-user account, no route to Root but a referendum, and every privilege on that short
   list checkable in the runtime metadata (Section 12).
 • Fair launch, honest non-goals. The project holds no pre-mined balance and earns fees by
   mining like anyone else; v1 does not claim FPGA/ASIC resistance, external usefulness, or
   recognition-based asset identity.


3 System architecture
Numen is a Substrate solochain [1] organized in five layers (Figure 1). The consensus layer
couples a PoW import pipeline with a GRANDPA voter; the runtime hosts the chain logic as
FRAME pallets (balances, validator management, sessions, online heartbeats, ASERT diﬀiculty,
treasury and referenda governance, the prime upgrade pallet, on-chain identity, vesting, and the
PowVerifyApi runtime API that re-executes PoScan seals); the EVM layer embeds Frontier for full
Ethereum compatibility. A public network of a few thousand nodes is the design target.
Three node roles emerge from configuration, not protocol: mining nodes (built-in miner or mining
RPC enabled), validator nodes (session keys registered, coins locked or exempt), and plain full /
RPC nodes. All of them verify everything; none of them trusts any other.

4.1 Block production

Blocks are produced competitively by PoScan mining at a target interval of 10 seconds, with a
hard minimum spacing of 2 seconds, enforced on the timestamp rather than on arrival. Block
construction is capped at 2 s, and import verification runs on a 5 s budget, keeping propagation-
induced orphan rates within the budget of Section 14.1. The block author is the miner address
configured on the producing node; it receives the block reward (initially 16 NUMN, halving; Sec-
tion 11) plus its fee share and all tips. Orphaned and uncle blocks receive nothing: with 10-second
blocks and fast finality, uncle accounting adds complexity without measurable fairness gain.

4.2 Fork choice

 • Heaviest chain. The best chain is the one with the highest accumulated diﬀiculty, not the
   greatest height.
 • Finality dominates weight. Any branch conflicting with a GRANDPA-finalized block never
   becomes best, whatever its accumulated diﬀiculty. This rule is enforced in the block-import
   pipeline and is the load-bearing joint of the hybrid design (Section 14.3).
 • First-seen tie-break. Among equal-weight heads, a node keeps the chain it saw first, blunting
   selfish-mining release games.

4.3 GRANDPA finality and sessions

GRANDPA runs as an independent layer above PoW. Validators vote on chains rather than indi-
vidual blocks; a round concluding with more than 2/3 of validator weight finalizes an entire prefix
at once, so bursts of blocks are absorbed gracefully. With gossip_duration = 2 s a voting round
completes within about 10 s, and a transaction goes from inclusion to irreversibility in about a
minute. Finalized blocks are irreversible: no reorganization can cross them.
The validator set changes only at session boundaries (every 60 blocks, roughly 10 minutes).
Joins and exits registered during a session take effect at the next boundary through GRANDPA’s
ScheduledChange mechanism, which lets the old set finish its last round before the handover (Sec-
tion 14.3). One GRANDPA vote can finalize many blocks, so finality throughput is never the
bottleneck.

4.4 Two-layer security model

Safety rests on two independent thresholds. The unfinalized frontier is protected by PoW: rewrit-
ing it requires a majority of hash power (>50%). Finalized history is protected by BFT: reverting
it requires corrupting more than 1/3 of validator weight (Section 7.1). An attacker must beat both
layers to rewrite settled history, and each layer alone degrades gracefully: losing GRANDPA
(validators offline) leaves a functioning pure-PoW chain without finality; losing PoW majority still
cannot touch finalized blocks. If the validator set empties out (everyone exited or kicked), the
chain degrades to pure PoW until new validators lock in and GRANDPA resumes at the next
session boundary.

5.1 Seed derivation: binding the work


                           seed = SHA-256(protocol ∥ 0x00 ∥ pre_hash ∥ nonce)
Binding pre_hash makes work block-specific: nothing can be precomputed before the parent is
known, and nothing can be replayed onto another block. The miner address is not a separate
field in the seal; it is committed in the block’s pre-runtime digest, which is part of the pre-image
of pre_hash. Changing the address changes the digest, hence pre_hash, hence the seed, hence
the entire asteroid and the work: observing another miner’s candidate nonce is worthless, and
the address that seeds the work is the same one that receives the reward.
The seed is the full 32-byte digest, and this width is a security parameter, not a convenience.
If the seed were truncated, the work domain would be capped at the truncated width, and a
capped domain is enumerable: a miner scans it once offline, keeps the seeds whose work clears
diﬀiculty, then reaches them by hashing nonces instead of scanning. The advantage of such a
table scales as (N/2w ) × (Cscan /Chash ) for table size N and seed width w; diﬀiculty cancels out
of the ratio, so no retarget corrects for it, and the table appreciates with the value of the chain.
At w = 256 the table cannot be built. The generator chain accepts the full digest end to end
(Pcg64::from_seed, the noise field, the permutation shuffle), because a full-width seed is only as
strong as the narrowest stage that consumes it.
The protocol tag enters the seed for the same reason it enters the work value: two protocol revi-
sions must not share an asteroid space, or generate-and-scan labour spent under one revision
would replay under the other for the price of a bucket hash.

5.2 Deterministic asteroid generation

asteroid() expands the seed through a PCG64 RNG into the asteroid’s scalar parameters (elon-
gation ratios, crater placement) and, critically, re-seeds a fresh fractional-Brownian-motion (fBm)
noise field for every seed, then displaces an icosphere refined to subdivision 4 (2,562 vertices).
Resolution is pinned by protocol: a miner cannot lower the mesh density, and cannot supply a
mesh at all; the generator is the only entry to the pipeline. That kills the trivial-object and forgery
attacks of Section 1.3 by construction.
The work counts are pinned too: 5 noise octaves, 10 craters, 4 lobes, all protocol constants that
the seed cannot vary. If per-seed cost were drawn from the seed, a miner could peek at the
cheap parameters first and drop expensive seeds before scanning, paying less than one scan
per nonce on average. Constant counts make every nonce cost the same, so there is nothing to
filter.

5.3 Spectral scan: the work itself

The mesh is scanned by the spectral3d public API [5]: the shape is normalized (translation
and scale removed, the features rotation-invariant by construction), its surface sampled at 4,096
points, and condensed into a 23-dimensional spectrum: 2 covariance eigenvalue ratios, a mean-
radius statistic, 6 area-weighted and 6 radius-weighted spherical-harmonic power values (orders
1–6), and an 8-bin radius histogram. This is genuine geometric computation (sampling, covari-
ance, spherical harmonics) executed once per nonce; a million attempts are a million scans. The
sample count is protocol-pinned, like the mesh resolution.

5.4 Fine quantization: restoring full entropy



s = SHA-256(protocol ∥ 0x00 ∥ pre_hash ∥ bkts),        bkts[i] = round(feats[i]/(STEP[i] ⋅ εscale ))

This stage is where the analysis of Section 1.3 lands. STEP is spectral3d’s identity-grade bucket
width. Its coarseness is what collapses a recognition work space. PoScan multiplies it by εscale =
10−5 (per-dimension quantum ≈ 10−6 ), far below the noise floor, so neighbouring seeds land in
different buckets across 19–21 of the 23 dimensions and the bucket vector inherits the full chaos
of the per-seed noise field. Hashing the concatenated integer buckets with the protocol string
and pre_hash yields s, uniform over 256 bits. pre_hash enters here directly, not only through the
seed, so work stays block-specific even if a future generator revision narrows its internal state.
A scanned seed table never outlives one block. The diﬀiculty judgment (s × difficulty must not
overflow U256) is the standard Substrate PoW comparison, unchanged. The protocol string
(Appendix A) is a pure version token; any rule change bumps it, throwing old and new work into
disjoint spaces (a clean fork point).

5.5 Seal and verification

The seal stays {nonce, work}: bucket vectors and meshes never travel. A verifier replays the
entire path (derive seed, regenerate mesh, rescan, requantize, rehash) and accepts if and only if
the recomputed s equals seal.work and meets the diﬀiculty. Verification costs exactly one scan,
measured at roughly 4 ms native and 6.5 ms inside the wasm runtime. The miner pays millions
of them, so PoW’s asymmetry survives inside the 5 s import budget. The miner address used
in seed derivation is read from the block’s author digest at import, so the binding holds without

5.6 Why shortcuts fail

PoScan’s work claim is only as strong as the absence of a cheaper path to s. Two shortcut
families were attacked experimentally and closed by parameter choice; three more are closed by
construction.

 • Low-resolution proxies (measured). At identity-grade buckets, a cheap decimated mesh
   reproduces the winning bucket vector 65–100% of the time; the scan could be faked. At
   εscale ≤ 10−4 the hit rate is measured zero across every decimation tried; the floor is set by
   sampling drift (512 vs 4,096 samples drift ∼ 10−5 ), hence ε ∼ 10−6 with margin.
 • Surrogate models (measured). Predicting features from the seed’s scalar parameters with-
   out evaluating the noise field fails because the fBm field is re-cast per seed: swapping only
   the fBm implementation jitters 19–21 of 23 dimensions beyond the quantum. Computing the
   features requires evaluating the true field at mesh resolution, which is exactly the work being
   avoided.
 • Pre-scan tables (by construction). Scan a seed domain offline once, keep the winners, then
   mine by hashing nonces into the table, with the scan bypassed entirely and mining security
   degraded to SHA-256 throughput. Closed twice over: the full 256-bit seed makes the domain
   unenumerable, and pre_hash inside the work hash means whatever table existed dies with
   the next block.
 • Cost-peeking filters (by construction). Skip expensive seeds by peeking at cheap genera-
   tor parameters first. Closed by pinning every work count as a protocol constant (Section 5.2).
 • Self-chosen meshes (by construction). The generator is seed-locked and verifiers regen-
   erate (Section 5.2).

6 Diﬀiculty regulation: ASERT with outage re-anchoring

6.1 Absolutely scheduled exponential targets

Diﬀiculty follows ASERT [4] (the aserti3-2d family): the next target is computed directly from how
far the chain is ahead of or behind its absolute schedule, measured against a fixed anchor block.

                                                next_target = anchor_target × 2 (Δt−Tideal ⋅Δh)/H

with Tideal = 10 s and halflife H = 1800 s (about 180 blocks): every 30 minutes of schedule lag
halves diﬀiculty, every 30 minutes of schedule lead doubles it (Figure 5a). Because each target
is computed absolutely from the anchor rather than recursively from the parent, the oscillation
and cumulative rounding drift of window-based retarget algorithms disappear. All arithmetic is
integer fixed-point: 2x is approximated by a 16-bit fixed-point routine (integer shift plus a cubic
polynomial, error < 0.013%) whose coeﬀicients are consensus-frozen, and the target saturates
into [1, U256::MAX] rather than overflowing or reaching zero. Within that range diﬀiculty moves
exactly as the schedule demands; there is no per-step clamp, rate limit, or frequency cap to
game. The anchor initializes at block #1 (genesis carries no wall-clock timestamp); on schedule,
consecutive diﬀiculty changes stay within regular fluctuation bounds.
ASERT trusts timestamps, so timestamps are constrained: a block’s timestamp may run at most
2 s ahead of network time, and it must advance at least 2 s past its parent’s or the block fails
import. Within that window, the worst-case timestamp manipulation moves diﬀiculty by 0.077%,
economically irrelevant against the 1,800 s halflife.

6.2 Recovery from hash-power collapse

Miners query a real-time diﬀiculty that evaluates ASERT against the wall clock, so if hash power
vanishes the effective diﬀiculty decays continuously. After 90% of hash power leaves, the chain
resumes block production within about an hour (the availability target), with no special-case code.
A pure fixed anchor, however, treats downtime as schedule debt to be repaid in blocks: after a
one-day outage the chain would owe thousands of near-free blocks. That is height spam with
no economic meaning. The protocol therefore re-anchors on interruption: whenever the gap
between consecutive blocks exceeds 1,800 s, the anchor moves to the recovery block (its target,
timestamp, height). Height debt vanishes; if hash power has returned, blocks arrive faster than
schedule and ASERT presses diﬀiculty back to equilibrium within regular bounds (Figure 5b).
The recovery block itself is mined at the decayed diﬀiculty. The protocol accepts that as bounded

7 GRANDPA and validators
GRANDPA supplies the finality; validators supply the GRANDPA. The algorithm is an agreement,
not a shield: every guarantee it makes is borrowed from the permissioned set that does the
voting. Validators are the accountable half of the protocol, and this section is about keeping their
set sound.

7.1 GRANDPA’s fragility

GRANDPA carries no economic weight of its own. Every active validator holds exactly one vote
and the set is capped at 1,000, so finality is decided by headcount rather than by stake. The set
is therefore the entire attack surface, and it fails in three distinct ways. Lose more than 1/3 of the
validators to downtime and no round can reach 2/3, so finality stops while PoW keeps producing
blocks. Against 10 honest validators, an attacker who enrols 5 more and parks them offline does
exactly that without casting a vote. Corrupt more than 1/3 and the same arithmetic becomes a
safety fault, since two conflicting prefixes can each gather enough votes. Corrupt more than 2/3
and the attacker finalizes whatever it wants, because finality outranks accumulated diﬀiculty and
honest nodes will follow. Against the same 10, that takes 21 seats. Hash power cannot repair
any of these. What guards the set is who gets in and who stays live.

7.2 Why entry is by invitation

The arithmetic above bites hardest at launch, when the 2/3 that captures finality is a handful of
seats. Suppose entry were open, gated by an affordable stake, the standard recipe. Mining is
the only issuance, so in the chain’s first years the large holders are, by construction, the miner
interest bloc; whatever a seat costs, the mining bloc is the first and for a long time the only party
able to pay for enough of them. A bloc that locks its way to 2/3 of a small set holds both halves of
consensus at once. It produces the blocks and it finalizes them, and a group that writes history
and also ratifies it can settle whatever ledger it likes.
Numen closes this from three sides. Staked entry is closed for the early years, and closed the
simple way: no switch and no special mechanism, just a lock threshold set beyond capture, with a
capturing share of seats costing more than the entire genesis issuance. The working entrance is
an invitation instead: centrally reviewed, deliberately so, the same passage many networks made
through a permissioned early phase. And the airdrop works the distribution side, spreading vote-
weight across a far broader group (Section 11).

7.3 Is the invitation era another monopoly?

Call it what it is: centralized control over who validates. Two facts bound what that control can
do. First, entry control is a security parameter, not an economic one; the gate decides who may
vote in GRANDPA and touches nothing else, no balance, no referendum, no identity. Second,
the process is public: the criteria below are published, anyone who meets them can apply in the
open, and every grant and removal on the invitation list is an on-chain event the community can

audit. The gate exists to stop a monopoly on finality, not to hand prime one, and motoz commits
to never abusing it.
If the word “centralized” still repels you, that instinct is healthy. But weigh it against Section 7.2: an
open door at launch does not produce decentralization, it produces the surface of one, referenda
and finality both held in fact by the bloc with the coins. You must choose: trust the founder, or
trust the bloc. When the invitation era ends is Section 13.3.

7.4 Who gets invited

Entry has two gates, both checked on-chain. First, a qualified on-chain identity (Section 10),
meaning a judged identity with a reachable plaintext social channel. Second, an invitation from
prime. Provided the set is not full and the account is not in a cooldown, the candidate joins the
active set at the next session boundary.
prime looks for four things in a candidate.

 • An active member of the community.
 • Trusted by the community.
 • Unaﬀiliated with mining pools and large miners.
 • Willing to pay for a well-connected server.

prime maintains an on-chain stake-exemption list (set_stake_exempt, every change a public event):
listed accounts join with a zero-amount lock, and the other gates (identity, session key, capacity,
cooldown) stay in force. Genesis validators are enrolled automatically at genesis and may re-
enter stake-free until removed; an exempt validator’s lifecycle is otherwise identical to a staked
one’s, so the machinery has no second code path. The exemption doubles as liveness insurance
against the 1/3 stall of Section 7.1, since the list lets the project backfill validators in an incident
without friction. It touches nothing else; exempt validators cannot spend, cannot slash, cannot
alter any account.

7.5 Two keys, never mixed

A validator operates two keys with sharply separate roles. The account key holds the funds,
carries the stake and the on-chain identity, and takes any consequences; it signs only three on-
chain actions, registering session keys, locking to join, and requesting exit. It stays offline, never
on the server. The session keys (GRANDPA and im-online) are the working keys the node signs
with continuously; the node generates them on the server and their private halves never leave it.
They hold no assets: a compromised server loses only the “mask,” and the operator re-registers
a fresh set with the offline account key while the stake stays untouched. This is why a mainnet
validator can run on modest hardware while keeping custody of a large lock entirely offline.

mittee. Detected GRANDPA equivocation (double-voting in prevote or precommit) triggers the
same eviction path, with the equivocation record kept public and permanent.

7.7 No slashing and no reward, by design

Neither offline eviction nor equivocation destroys locked funds, and validators earn no protocol
reward: block rewards go to miners, and fees split between miners and the treasury. These two
facts are one deliberate design, not two concessions.
No reward is what makes no-slashing coherent. If validating paid a yield, the network would
attract operators chasing that yield. They would cut node specifications to maximize margin and
run finality on the cheapest hardware available. A finality committee of under-provisioned, profit-
trimmed boxes is exactly what a 10-second chain cannot tolerate. Removing the reward removes
that incentive at the root: the only people who lock a large sum for months to run demanding
infrastructure with no financial return are those who want the network to work. Running a validator
takes real operational skill; pricing it in altruism rather than yield filters for that skill and against
the race to the bottom. And with nothing to earn, there is nothing a slash needs to protect, so no
honest operator can lose capital to a misconfigured firewall or a transient outage.
The deterrents are therefore exclusion and time, not confiscation: an evicted validator stops
earning influence immediately, keeps its coins locked until the original expiry, and cannot re-
join for a one-day cooldown. This trades the sharper crypto-economic edge of slashing for a
materially lower barrier to honest participation, which suits a young network whose finality-safety
margin is capacity, not stake-at-risk. Finality safety still requires that less than 1/3 of validator
weight be malicious. As the network matures, fee-sharing or explicit finality rewards may be
revisited through governance, as an honest “not yet” rather than a permanent “never.”

7.8 Lock mechanics

Locked coins cannot be transferred or unlocked before expiry. For active validators the system
renews the lock automatically once per day, so honest operation never lapses; eviction or a
voluntary request_exit() stops auto-renewal, after which the lock simply runs out and the funds
free themselves. There is no early unlock, even after exit, and the same holds for a candidate
who locked but never entered the set (session key missing, or the set full): the lock runs to its
original expiry with auto-renewal stopped, never refunded early. Re-joining after an exit is always
a fresh lock under the standard conditions; where an old record exists, the stricter value wins;
the unlock height only moves later, and the locked amount only grows.

12 Governance and treasury

12.1 OpenGov and the treasury

Numen runs OpenGov (pallet-referenda with pallet-conviction-voting). Holders vote on trea-
sury spending, on runtime code, on who may judge identities and grant usernames, and on
whether another referendum gets to finish. Voters lock for conviction-weighted power (1× to
6×, with a 7-day base lock) and may delegate per track; proposal call-bodies are stored via
pallet-preimage and executed by pallet-scheduler at the enactment block. Submitting a ref-
erendum requires a qualified identity (Section 10) and a 5 NUMN deposit: a judged, reachable
identity stands behind every proposal. That prices out drive-by spam without pricing out peo-
ple. Nine tracks carry the vote, each with its own periods, deposit, and pass curves (Table 3).

Support (turnout) thresholds are measured against active issuance, not total supply: the trea-
sury’s own holdings are marked as deactivated issuance each block and excluded from the de-
nominator, so the coin that does not vote cannot dilute the voters’ support ratio. At genesis the
denominator is roughly the 50M airdrop float and grows with mining. Because a treasury payout
moves funds from deactivated to active, the more the treasury spends, the larger the denominator
and the higher the bar for the next spend. The damper is built in.

12.2 Treasury culture: deliver, then pay

Community voting decides treasury spending, but voters are not auditors and cannot catch every
scam at the ballot. Numen therefore leans on structure rather than voter vigilance. Payouts flow
through bounties and child-bounties: a proposal is approved for a scope, a curator is appointed
against a forfeitable bond, work is delivered and verified, and only then are funds released. De-
liver first, pay later. Curators can open child-bounties within a parent’s budget and appoint sub-
curators without returning to a full referendum, so large programmes scale without re-voting every
line item, while the per-track ceilings of Table 3 cap the blast radius of any single mistake. Risk
is contained by how the funds move, not by assuming the crowd is expert.


13 Sudo and prime
pallet-sudo is the last centralized key in a Substrate runtime: it can dispatch anything as Root,
and for as long as it exists, on-chain governance is decorative. Numen removes it outright. What
remains is a handful of downgraded privileges kept for early-network safety, called prime. A single
prime key is written at genesis and can be rotated by its holder via set_key, which emits an on-
chain event. Most of what prime holds is shared with a referendum track, so prime is the fast
path rather than the only one. The treasury veto, the validator stake-exemption list, and the right

13.2 Why these privileges sit with prime

Every class of prime privilege invites the same objection: surely this belongs to a referendum.
The answer differs per class, so we take them in turn, stating the reasons plainly rather than
hiding them.
Runtime upgrade.

1. The runtime has one author. Numen’s runtime comes from one repository, and nobody else
   ships one. Opening submission to everyone adds no proposer worth having, only a standing
   invitation to put hostile code on the ballot. prime puts up what the project built.
2. Holders decide whether it lands. The code goes on public record and holders have the
   decision period to vote it down. prime opens the vote and nothing more.

The brakes.
Cancel and kill sit with prime, and holders reach both by referendum. prime’s hand is the fast
one. Neither an attack nor a live exploit waits out a vote.

13.3 The path to decentralization is left open

None of this is a permanent settlement. Moving the upgrade privilege off prime now takes only
a submission gate, since the track already runs and opening it past prime is a one-line origin
change. The validator gate opens the same way. As the set grows, governance lowers the
100M entry threshold until invitations stop mattering (Section 7.4). The road is left paved so that
decentralizing later is a small patch, not a rewrite.
Each privilege has its own floor conditions. Meeting them opens the handover discussion, not
the handover itself; the judgment that no metric can capture, whether vote-weight has genuinely
dispersed, stays in the open discussion where it belongs.

 • Opening the upgrade track. Submission goes to a foundation or a standing technical body
   once one exists. No date is set.
 • Retiring prime’s brakes. The community puts it to an on-chain vote and decides.
 • The validator gate. One month of mainnet operation, a healthy PoW layer, and a large
   enough validator count. A healthy PoW layer means hash rate spread widely enough that no
   single miner or pool comes near 50%. How large the validator count must be is still open; it
   needs discussion with security experts in the community, and anything below 10 is out of the
   question.
 • Identity registrars. prime loses the privilege at the first runtime upgrade after it appoints the
   automatic registrar.
 • The rest. The treasury and bounty vetoes sit with prime only because removing sudo left
   those origins without a holder. Nothing uses them today. The day something does, a new
   track takes them and prime steps back.

14.3 Known risks

We state the open problems as plainly as the design.

 • No FPGA/ASIC resistance in v1 (accepted). PoScan is compute-bound with small state;
   specialized hardware will outperform GPUs. v1 accepts this openly. If concentration threat-
   ens, a v2 memory-hard layer (an Autolykos-style [7] multi-GB table with random access) is
   a separate, planned design; the runtime-upgradeable verifier makes the switch forkless in
   mechanism, though it remains a hard fork in work semantics. One distinction matters here:
   hardware that scans faster runs an eﬀiciency race we accept, while a path that skips scanning
   would break the work function outright. The pre-scan table was the latter, and it is closed by
   seed width (Section 5.6); no future revision may re-narrow the seed.
 • Centralized early validator entry (transitional). During the invitation era, who validates is
   decided by the prime-held exemption list. This is deliberate (the alternative at launch is a
   finality set purchasable for a rounding error of genesis), but it is a centralization of liveness,
   stated as such. The exit is the governance-lowered threshold of Section 13.3.
 • Integration seams (tested). The finality-respecting fork choice must be airtight in the import
   pipeline; set handovers at session boundaries must let the old GRANDPA set finish its final
   round; an empty validator set degrades the chain to pure PoW until new locks arrive. Each
   seam has dedicated tests.
 • PoW algorithm replacement is a hard fork (accepted). Even via runtime upgrade, switch-
   ing work semantics partitions miners; activation heights must be planned and announced.
   The protocol-string mechanism (Section 5.4) makes the partition explicit and clean.
 • prime key custody (live risk). Enough compromised signer keys put arbitrary code on the
   ballot (Section 13.2), and the same custody protects the gate privileges (validator invitations,

15 Risk factors
The following risks are material and stated without softening.

 • Immature software, no audit. The protocol is under active development, and no third-party
   security audit has been performed. Bugs, including consensus-level bugs, are possible.
 • Mining centralization. v1 does not resist FPGA/ASIC hardware, so hash power may concen-
   trate and leave the PoW layer less decentralized than commodity mining would (Section 14.3).
 • Finality depends on altruistic, initially invited validators. Validators earn no reward and
   early entry is by invitation, so finality liveness rests on enough capable operators choosing to
   serve. Too few, and finality stalls while the chain continues as pure PoW (Section 14.3).
 • Upgrade-key compromise. Compromise of enough prime signer keys would put arbitrary
   code on the ballot. The attacker still has to win the referendum, so the exposure is a thinly at-
   tended vote rather than a signature, and no mitigation removes that risk entirely (Section 14.3).
 • Identity is public by design. Referendum submitters and validators must expose a judged
   identity with a plaintext social channel. That accountability floor is deliberate; participants who
   require anonymity should mine or run full nodes instead.
 • Market and liquidity. NUMN may have volatile or no market value.
 • Regulatory and legal uncertainty. Treatment of tokens and public blockchains varies by
   jurisdiction and may change adversely. The project is maintained anonymously; there is no
   company standing behind the network and no entity from which to seek recourse.
